## Who This Trip Is Best For

This itinerary theme suits travelers who want their trip to have some depth beyond photo stops — people interested in Shinto and Buddhist history, architecture, or simply the feeling of a place that's been considered significant for centuries. It also works well for travelers who want a natural way to slow down a fast-paced Japan itinerary, since several of these sites (especially Koyasan and Ise) reward unhurried time rather than a quick visit.

It's less suited to a trip built entirely around nightlife, shopping, or a tightly packed multi-city checklist — you'll get more out of this list if you're willing to give at least one or two of these places a half day or more.

## Best Time to Visit

Most of these sites are open and meaningful year-round, since they're working religious sites rather than seasonal attractions. That said:

- **Spring and autumn** are the most comfortable for the walking involved, and add cherry blossoms or foliage at sites like Kiyomizu-dera and Nikko Toshogu.
- **Summer** in Japan is hot and humid; sites with extensive outdoor walking (Fushimi Inari's full mountain loop, Koyasan, Nara Park) are more demanding in July and August.
- **Winter** can be atmospheric, especially Kinkaku-ji after snowfall, but Koyasan gets cold and can see significant snow.

See our [guide to Japan through the four seasons](/seasons/japan-four-seasons/) for a broader look at how timing affects a Japan trip.

## Suggested Route

If you're building a first-time Japan itinerary around this list, the most efficient grouping is:

1. **Kanto leg (Tokyo-based):** Meiji Jingu, with Nikko Toshogu as a day trip or overnight.
2. **Kansai leg (Kyoto/Osaka/Nara-based):** Fushimi Inari Taisha, Kiyomizu-dera, and Kinkaku-ji in Kyoto; Todai-ji in Nara; Ise Jingu as a day trip; Koyasan as an overnight detour.
3. **Chugoku leg (if time allows):** Itsukushima Shrine near Hiroshima, and Izumo Taisha further along the Sanin coast for travelers with extra time.

A first-time, two-week Japan trip can realistically cover the Kanto and Kansai legs; the Chugoku leg suits a return trip or a longer itinerary.

## Practical Tips

- Shoes come off at some indoor halls — slip-on shoes make this easier.
- Coins are useful for offering boxes at shrine and temple entrances.
- Many sites have specific etiquette for prayer (how many bows, claps, and bows again) posted near the entrance — it's fine to watch and follow other visitors.

> Opening hours, admission fees, and seasonal closures vary by site and change over time. Confirm current details on each shrine or temple's official website, or through a reputable current travel resource, before finalizing your itinerary.

## Cultural Etiquette

A few habits apply broadly across Shinto shrines and Buddhist temples in Japan:

- Pause briefly and bow slightly before passing through a torii gate or temple gate — you're entering sacred ground.
- At the temizuya (water basin), rinse both hands and then your mouth before approaching the main hall, using the ladle provided.
- Keep voices low inside halls and cemetery areas, particularly at active pilgrimage sites like Koyasan.
- Ask before photographing monks, priests, or ongoing ceremonies.

## Where to Stay

For the Kansai leg of this list, staying in central Kyoto puts you within reach of Fushimi Inari, Kiyomizu-dera, and Kinkaku-ji, with Nara and Ise as day trips. For Koyasan, staying overnight in a shukubo (temple lodging) on the mountain is a core part of the experience, not just a place to sleep — it typically includes a shared vegetarian dinner and the option to join morning prayers.

## Things to Book

Most of these sites don't require advance tickets, but a few things are worth arranging ahead of time:

- **Shukubo lodging at Koyasan**, which has limited capacity and fills up during peak seasons.
- **Transportation passes** if your route spans multiple regions — regional and national rail passes can be worth comparing against individual tickets depending on your route.
- **Guided tours**, if you'd like historical or religious context beyond what's posted on-site, particularly at Ise Jingu and Koyasan.
