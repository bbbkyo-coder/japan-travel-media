## Who This Trip Is Best For

This is a strong itinerary theme for travelers who plan trips around meals as much as sights — people who'd rather build a day around a specific dish than a specific monument. It also rewards travelers willing to venture beyond Tokyo and Kyoto, since several of the most distinctive food regions (Hokkaido, Fukuoka, Okinawa) sit outside the standard first-time route.

If you have significant dietary restrictions, some regions on this list (Kyoto's shojin ryori tradition especially) will suit you better than others — see the FAQ below.

## Best Time to Visit

Food seasonality is a real factor in Japan, more so than in many countries:

- **Winter** brings peak seafood in Hokkaido and Kanazawa, including crab season.
- **Autumn** is harvest season in Yamanashi and Nagano, and a good time for mushroom and chestnut dishes nationwide.
- **Summer** favors Okinawa's warm-climate cuisine, though it's also typhoon season there.
- Kyoto's kaiseki menus shift with the seasons more visibly than almost any other food tradition in Japan.

Our [guide to Japan through the four seasons](/seasons/japan-four-seasons/) covers the broader seasonal picture beyond food.

## Suggested Route

A realistic food-focused itinerary for a first or second trip:

1. **Kansai base (4-5 days):** Osaka for street food, Kyoto for refined and traditional cuisine, with Nara as a side trip.
2. **Tokyo (3-4 days):** The widest range in one city, useful for sampling regional specialties you might not otherwise reach.
3. **One further region (2-3 days):** Fukuoka for ramen and yatai, Hiroshima for okonomiyaki and oysters, or Kanazawa for seafood — pick based on what's on your must-eat list.

Hokkaido and Okinawa are worth dedicating a separate, focused trip to rather than squeezing into a first-time itinerary, given the distances involved.

## Practical Tips

- Many of the best local spots are small, with limited seating — arriving right at opening or slightly before typical meal times can help avoid a wait.
- Cash is still useful in smaller, older establishments, even as card and mobile payment acceptance has grown.
- Department store basement food halls (depachika) are a reliable, high-quality option in any major city when you don't have a specific reservation.

> Restaurant recommendations, prices, and hours change frequently. Treat any specific restaurant name you find (including in blog posts or older guides) as a starting point to verify, not a guarantee it's still open or unchanged.

## Cultural Etiquette

- It's polite to say "itadakimasu" before eating and "gochisosama" after finishing, even as a visitor attempting a little Japanese.
- Pouring your own drink is considered less polite than pouring for others at the table and letting them pour for you.
- Tipping is not customary in Japan and can cause confusion — good service is already the standard, not something to pay extra for.
- Slurping noodles, including ramen and soba, is normal and not considered rude.

## Where to Stay

For a food-focused Kansai trip, staying in central Osaka or Kyoto puts you within a short train ride of both cities plus Nara. In Tokyo, staying near a specific neighborhood that matches your food interests (for example, Tsukiji/Ginza for seafood and sushi, or Shibuya/Shinjuku for izakaya culture) can be more useful than picking a hotel based on sightseeing alone.

## Things to Book

- **High-demand restaurant reservations**, especially sushi counters in Tokyo and well-known kaiseki restaurants in Kyoto, which can require booking weeks in advance.
- **Food tours or market tours**, which can be a good way to get context and recommendations quickly in an unfamiliar city.
- **Regional transportation**, if your food route spans multiple regions like Kansai plus Hiroshima or Kanazawa.
